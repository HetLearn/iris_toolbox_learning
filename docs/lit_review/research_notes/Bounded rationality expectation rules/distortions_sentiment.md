# Belief distortions, sentiment and model uncertainty in DSGE expectation formation (with a sweep of remaining bounded-rationality families)

Notation (hetlearn): for a chosen forward-looking variable x, the model term `E_t x_{t+1}` is replaced by `x_exp1_t = n1_t·E^RE_t x_{t+1} + n2_t·E^BR_t x_{t+1}`. "Wedge" means an additive term `w_t` with its own exogenous process, added to `x_exp1_t` or to one type's forecast. Formulas quoted from sources keep the source's notation; derivations under "Inferences" are mine and should be checked before use. Compiled 2026-10-20. Several numbers come from working-paper versions; each such case is flagged.

## 1. Robust control and model uncertainty (Hansen–Sargent multiplier preferences)

### Takeaway
In the linear-quadratic case the robust agent forecasts with the approximating model plus a worst-case shock mean that is linear in the state, `w_{t+1} = K̂x_t` with `K̂ = (θI − C'P̂C)^{-1}C'P̂(A − BF̂)`: the drift is the shock loading times the value-function matrix, i.e. proportional to the value-function gradient. In a nonlinear DSGE solved at first order a constant θ only shifts constants, so time variation needs a state-dependent θ_t rescaled with shock volatility (the BBH perturbation); the US applications I found are Hansen–Sargent–Tallarini (consumption and investment), Szőke (yields plus survey forecasts) and BBH (calibrated to Michigan wedges), and I found no estimated Smets–Wouters-type model with multiplier robustness.

### Cited Findings
- Hansen & Sargent, "Robust Control and Model Uncertainty," *American Economic Review* 91(2), May 2001, pp. 60–66 (Papers & Proceedings) — [larspeterhansen.org listing](https://larspeterhansen.org/research/papers/page/8/)
- The book *Robustness* (Princeton University Press) is dated 2008 in the brief; Angeletos–Collard–Dellas list it as "2007. Robustness. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press", so the year differs across citations — [ACD, NBER WP 20807, reference list](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20807.pdf); catalogue record [oeaw.ac.at](https://oeaw.ac.at/resources/Record/9781400829385)
- Robust LQ problem (cost-minimisation form): `J(x) = min_u max_w { x'Rx + u'Qu + β[J(Ax + Bu + Cw) − θw'w] }` with `x_{t+1} = Ax_t + Bu_t + Cw_{t+1}`; `D(P) = P + PC(θI − C'PC)^{-1}C'P`; robust rule `F̂ = (Q + βB'D(P̂)B)^{-1}βB'D(P̂)A`; worst case `K̂ = (θI − C'P̂C)^{-1}C'P̂(A − BF̂)`, `w_{t+1} = K̂x_t`; in the stochastic case the worst-case shock distribution is `N(K̂x_t, (I − θ^{-1}C'P̂C)^{-1})`; as θ → ∞, D → identity, K̂ → 0 and the standard LQ solution returns; θ must exceed a breakdown point (`I − C'P̂C/θ` with nonnegative eigenvalues) — [QuantEcon, "Robustness"](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/robustness.html)
- General (non-LQ) recursion used by BBH: `v_t = min_m { u(x_t) + βE_t[m v_{t+1}] + (β/θ_t)E_t[m log m] }`, `E_t[m] = 1`; optimal distortion `m*_{t+1} ∝ exp(−θ_t v_{t+1})`; risk-sensitive form `v_t = u(x_t) − (β/θ_t) log E_t exp(−θ_t v_{t+1})`; with log utility this is Epstein–Zin with `γ_t = θ_t + 1`, and survey forecasts separate pessimism from risk premia, which asset prices alone cannot — [QuantEcon lecture on BBH](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/subjective_beliefs_business_cycles.html)
- Linearised BBH solution: with `x_{t+1} = ψ_q + ψ_x x_t + ψ_w w_{t+1}`, the subjective mean of the shock is `−θ_t(v_x ψ_w)'` and the one-step wedge for `z_t = z̄'x_t` is `Δ_t(z) = −θ_t z̄'(ψ_wψ_w')v_x'` (scalar case: shock mean `ν_t = −θ_t v_x σ_x`); θ_t must be scaled at order 1/q with shock volatility or the distortion drops out under certainty equivalence; the policy matrices solve a modified Blanchard–Kahn system whose only change is an additive term −D, and θ̄ = 0 gives back RE; with constant θ the variance penalty affects only the constant `v_q`, not the slope — [QuantEcon lecture on BBH](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/subjective_beliefs_business_cycles.html)
- Giordani & Söderlind (2004), "Solution of macromodels with Hansen–Sargent robust policies: some extensions," *JEDC* 28(12): 2367–2397: methods for formulating and solving HS robust control problems, extended to discretion and simple rules, with Matlab, Octave and Gauss code; applications cover the term structure, time inconsistency of optimal monetary policy, the effect of expectations on inflation and output variance, and whether central banks should publish forecasts — [SWoPEc abstract](https://swopec.hhs.se/hastef/abs/hastef0499.htm); the method encompasses rational expectations as a special case — [SFU copy](https://www.sfu.ca/~kkasa/soderlin.pdf)
- Ellison & Sargent, "A Defense of the FOMC," is in *International Economic Review* 53(4), 2012, pp. 1047–1065, not the AER (earlier Oxford WP 457, 2009; CEPR DP 7510). FOMC forecasts are read as worst-case scenarios that a robust policymaker uses to choose decisions that hold up under misspecification, so they are not meant as predictions; this accounts for the Romer and Romer findings and for the differences between FOMC and Greenbook forecasts — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oxf:wpaper:457); [IDEAS, CEPR DP 7510](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/7510.html)
- Hansen, Sargent & Tallarini (1999), "Robust Permanent Income and Pricing," *REStud* 66(4): 873–907 — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v66y1999i4p873-907..html). Hansen, Sargent & Wang (2002, *Macroeconomic Dynamics*) describe it as a model that HST "estimated from time series on consumption and investment for the post-1970's United States", with an endowment that is the sum of two serially correlated components — [HSW 2002 PDF](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~dbackus/Exotic/1Robustness/HansenSargentWang%20MD%2002.pdf)
- Barillas, Hansen & Sargent (2009, *JET*), "Doubts or variability?": detection-error probabilities show that a moderate concern about misspecification under multiplier preferences can stand in for implausibly high risk aversion; the computation assumes log consumption is a random walk or trend-stationary with iid Gaussian errors (secondhand description) — [Okubo (2018), Tsukuba discussion paper](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/46570/files/1354.pdf)
- Bidder & Smith (2012), "Robust animal spirits," *JME* 59(8): 738–750: in an RBC model, fear of misspecification interacts with stochastic volatility to give time-varying worst cases; pessimism moves with aggregate volatility and amplifies volatility shocks — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/moneco/v59y2012i8p738-750.html); [FRBSF Economic Letter 2015-02](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2015/02/economic-volatility-business-cycles-animal-spirits/)
- Woodford (2010), "Robustly Optimal Monetary Policy with Near-Rational Expectations," *AER* 100(1): 274–303: the central bank guards against private expectations that deviate from model consistency within a bound; commitment still matters, the optimal long-run inflation target does not depend on the size of the allowed deviation, and optimal policy is more history-dependent than under RE — [NBER w11896](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11896); [SUFE listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/351174)
- Hansen & Sargent (2010), "Fragile beliefs and the price of uncertainty," *Quantitative Economics* 1(1): 129–162: Bayesian updating over several models combined with multiplier preferences; specification doubts tilt model probabilities pessimistically toward a long-run-risk model, which adds a countercyclical, history-dependent component to risk prices — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/quante/v1y2010i1p129-162.html); [Econometric Society](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/quantitative-economics/2010/07/01/Fragile-beliefs-and-the-price-of-uncertainty)
- Szőke (2022), "Estimating robustness," *JET* 199: 105225: a representative agent who fears misspecification of consumption and inflation persistence adopts a worst-case model, estimated from interest-rate and macro data through robust-control cross-equation restrictions; read as subjective beliefs, the implied interest-rate forecasts reproduce the dynamics and average level of bias in survey expectations — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jetheo/v199y2022ics0022053121000429.html)

### Inferences
- The "value-function gradient" reduced form follows from the inner maximisation: the first-order condition gives `θw = C'P̂x_{t+1}` at the worst-case next state, so the drift equals θ^{-1} times the shock loading C' times (half) the gradient of `x'P̂x`; solving out `x_{t+1}` gives the K̂ formula. The worst-case one-step forecast is `E^w_t x_{t+1} = E^0_t x_{t+1} + CK̂x_t`.
- The approximation order decides whether robustness creates first-order dynamics. With a quadratic value function (LQ) the gradient is linear in the state, so even a constant θ gives a state-dependent wedge `CK̂x_t`. With a first-order perturbation the value function is linear, its gradient is constant, and a constant θ gives only a constant wedge (a steady-state shift); BBH get time variation by making θ_t a state and rescaling it with volatility.
- For hetlearn the LQ route needs the agents' quadratic objective. For SW07 households that means a second-order approximation of utility under the current beliefs every quarter, then the Riccati iteration with D(P). Hard. A robust central bank (loss function given; Giordani–Söderlind code exists) is medium, but it distorts the policymaker's forecasts, not private expectations, unless private agents are assumed to share the bank's worst-case model.
- A survey-disciplined pessimistic type is easier through the BBH perturbation (Section 3) or the Ilut–Schneider worst-case mean (Section 2); both keep a linear Gaussian state space, so the time-varying Kalman filter likelihood still applies.

### Gaps
- HST (1999) point estimates and the robustness parameter value were not retrieved (the IDEAS page shows only an epigraph); I did not verify whether HST state an observational equivalence between robustness and the discount factor for quantity data.
- How Ellison–Sargent pick θ and which model they use were not verified.
- Barillas–Hansen–Sargent (2009) volume and pages not verified (my recollection, unverified: *JET* 144(6): 2388–2418).
- Not checked in this pass: Cogley, Colacito, Hansen & Sargent (2008, *JMCB*) on robustness and US monetary policy experimentation; Dennis (2008, *JEDC*); Leitemo & Söderström (2008, *Macroeconomic Dynamics*); Dennis, Leitemo & Söderström (2009, *JEDC*).
- No estimated medium-scale NK model with multiplier robustness on US data turned up; that is an absence in a short search, not proof that none exists.

## 2. Ambiguity aversion (Ilut–Schneider and successors)

### Takeaway
Maxmin ambiguity about the mean of an exogenous innovation has a clean linear reduced form: agents act as if the shock has mean −a_t, where a_t is an exogenous AR(1) "confidence" process; the equilibrium is the RE solution under that distorted law of motion, evaluated under the true process, so the one-step belief wedge on any variable is its impact response to the ambiguous innovation times −a_t (an additive wedge with a model-implied loading, plus a steady-state shift). Ilut–Schneider estimate this with standard Bayesian methods on US data; the published abstract reports that TFP and confidence shocks together explain about two-thirds of business-cycle variation in major aggregates, with confidence shocks about 70% of that.

### Cited Findings
- Ilut & Schneider (2014), "Ambiguous Business Cycles," *AER* 104(8): 2368–2399, DOI 10.1257/aer.104.8.2368: confidence shocks are changes in ambiguity about future TFP; the estimation uses the dispersion of survey forecasts about growth as a measure of confidence; TFP and confidence shocks jointly explain about two-thirds of business-cycle-frequency movements in major aggregates, and confidence shocks about 70% of that variation — [Duke repository record](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/2f8072fe-092c-4efa-9ead-2256ad5ac4b3); [SUFE listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/350743)
- January 2012 draft abstract: changes in ambiguity "work like changes in conditional means", so the models allow uncertainty shocks yet "can still be solved and estimated using first-order approximations"; a loss of confidence works like "unrealized" bad news — [NBER conference draft, Jan 2012](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf); [2011 WP, CMU](https://www.cmu.edu/tepper/faculty-and-research/assets/docs/amb_cycles.pdf)
- Belief set (2012 draft): `log Z_{t+1} = ρ_z log Z_t + σ_z ε_{t+1} + μ_t`, `μ_t ∈ [−a_t, −a_t + 2|a_t|]`, `a_{t+1} = (1 − ρ_a)ā + ρ_a a_t + σ_a ε^a_{t+1}`; agents know the a_t process but are unsure of the TFP mean; because the value function increases in Z, the worst case is the lower bound, so under the worst-case belief `log Z_{t+1} = ρ_z log Z_t + σ_z ε_{t+1} − a_t` — [NBER conference draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf)
- Solution (2012 draft): linearise around the "worst-case steady state" and solve the expectational difference equations under the worst-case belief p0 by undetermined coefficients, `ŵ⁰_t = ρŵ⁰_{t−1} + νŝ⁰_t`, without using the econometrician's density; around the "zero-risk steady state" the actual deviations follow the same `ŵ_t = ρŵ_{t−1} + νŝ_t` once displaced by actual shocks; the guess is verified by checking that the value function is increasing in Z; the linear Gaussian state space allows standard Bayesian estimation — [NBER conference draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf)
- Estimation in the 2012 draft: observables Δlog GDP, Δlog investment, Δlog consumption, log hours per capita, log gross inflation, log gross short rate, Δlog relative price of investment; US 1984Q1–2010Q1; no measurement error; `ā = nσ_z`, priors n ~ Beta(0.5, 0.25) and ρ_a ~ Beta(0.5, 0.15); the constraint `ā ≥ 3σ_a/√(1 − ρ_a²)` is imposed as binding — [NBER conference draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf)
- 2012 draft estimates (posterior mode): n = 0.963 and σ_z = 0.0045, so ā = 0.00435 (agents act as if next-quarter TFP is 0.435% lower); with ρ_z = 0.955 the worst-case long-run TFP is about 9% below the true mean; σ_a = 0.000405; through ambiguity, TFP fluctuations lower steady-state output by 15% and consumption by 16.4%, a welfare cost of about 13% of consumption — [NBER conference draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf)
- 2012 draft variance decomposition: at business-cycle frequencies (6–32 quarters) the ambiguity shock explains about 27% of GDP, 52% of consumption, 14% of investment, 31% of hours, 2% of inflation and 7% of the nominal rate; unconditionally 55%, 62%, 51%, 52%, 29% and 38%; in the linearised equilibrium `R_t = E^{p0}_t R^K_{t+1}`, so worst-case expected excess returns are zero while realised excess returns are countercyclical — [NBER conference draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf)
- Ilut & Saijo (2021), "Learning, confidence, and business cycles," *JME* 117: 354–376, DOI 10.1016/j.jmoneco.2020.01.010 (NBER WP 22958, 2016): heterogeneous firms face Knightian uncertainty about profitability and learn through production, so uncertainty rises in recessions and lowers confidence; the mechanism yields amplified hump-shaped dynamics, countercyclical labour and asset-market wedges and demand-driven comovement, is characterised in linear workhorse models, and in a rich model estimated on US macroeconomic and financial data reduces the need for standard real and nominal rigidities — [NBER w22958](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22958); [SUFE listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/274984)
- Bianchi, Ilut & Schneider (2018), "Uncertainty Shocks, Asset Supply and Pricing over the Business Cycle," *REStud* 85(2): 810–854, DOI 10.1093/restud/rdx035: an estimated business-cycle model with endogenous asset supply and ambiguity-averse investors; higher profit uncertainty lowers stock prices, debt and payouts, matching postwar comovement; ambiguity aversion permits a Markov-switching VAR representation — [NBER w20081](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20081); [SUFE listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/343922)
- Ilut, Krivenko & Schneider, "Uncertainty aversion and heterogeneous beliefs in linear models" (working paper; SED 2014 as "Uncertainty in linear models", SED 2015 under the current title; work in progress): a perturbation approach for linear dynamics when agents perceive exogenous shocks differently, including agents with different uncertainty aversion and so different worst-case beliefs; leading example is a borrower–lender model — [EconPapers, SED 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:red:sed014:857); [Krivenko's page](https://web.stanford.edu/~pavelkr)
- Masolo & Monti (2021), "Ambiguity, Monetary Policy and Trend Inflation," *JEEA* 19(2): 839–871, DOI 10.1093/jeea/jvaa020: ambiguity about central-bank behaviour in a simple NK model opens gaps between long-run inflation expectations, trend inflation and the target; ambiguity measured from Blue Chip survey data helps explain US long-run inflation expectations and trend inflation; optimal policy leans against pessimistic expectations but only partly undoes the effects — [publicatt](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/handle/10807/227728); [BoE WP 2015](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2015/ambiguity-monetary-policy-and-trend-inflation)
- Michelacci & Paciello (2020), "Ambiguous Policy Announcements," *REStud* 87(5): 2356–2398: ambiguity-averse households with heterogeneous net wealth judge forward-guidance credibility by a worst-case criterion; creditors weight announcements more, so forward guidance can be contractionary before implementation; calibrated to the euro area — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v87y2020i5p2356-2398..html)
- Baqaee (2020), "Asymmetric inflation expectations, downward rigidity of wages, and asymmetric business cycles," *JME* 114: 174–193: ambiguity-averse households overweight inflationary news, so wages rise fast and fall slowly and monetary policy has asymmetric effects — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/moneco/v114y2020icp174-193.html)
- Altug, Collard, Çakmaklı, Mukerji & Özsöylev (2020), "Ambiguous business cycles: A quantitative assessment," *RED* 38: 220–237: smooth-ambiguity (Klibanoff–Marinacci–Mukerji) RBC with irreversible investment and Kalman-filter learning; compared with Ilut–Schneider's maxmin NK model, the frictionless version fits quantity moments poorly, which the authors attribute to maxmin agents being too cautious and unresponsive to TFP — [TSE](https://www.tse-fr.eu/articles/ambiguous-business-cycles-quantitative-assessment)
- Review: NBER WP 29915, "Modeling Uncertainty as Ambiguity: a Review"; Ilut's Duke record lists the *Handbook of Economic Expectations* chapter as "Ambiguity", pp. 749–777, dated 2022 (title differs across sources) — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.Org/p/nbr/nberwo/29915.html); [Duke FDS](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/Economics/faculty/cli2/publications/369939)

### Inferences
- One-step wedge (my derivation from the IS solution): with `w_{t+1} = ρw_t + νs_{t+1}` and `s = (log Z, a)`, `E^{p0}_t w_{t+1} − E^{p*}_t w_{t+1} = −ν_Z a_t`, where ν_Z is the impact response of w to a unit TFP innovation. The ambiguity-averse forecast is the true-process forecast plus a wedge whose loading is the impact column of the ambiguous shock and whose driver is the exogenous AR(1) a_t. This is the "exogenous belief wedge process" reduced form the brief asks about. h-step wedges accumulate through the perceived TFP path (`−Σ_j ρ_z^{h−1−j} E_t a_{t+j}` in log Z units).
- In a linear model the impact responses to ε^Z do not depend on the a_t process, so ν_Z can be read off the current solution without a fixed point. hetlearn re-solves each quarter, so the loading would update with BR beliefs at no extra cost.
- hetlearn implementation: add a_t (AR(1), mean ā) to the state vector; for the ambiguity-averse group set `E^amb_t x_{t+1} = E^type_t x_{t+1} − B_Z a_t`, with B_Z the impact column of the TFP innovation in the current IRIS solution (rescaled to a unit-of-log-TFP innovation); optional measurement equation linking the SPF interquartile range of GDP growth to a_t (the published IS approach). Medium. The same template covers ambiguity about the policy shock (Masolo–Monti style; loading = impact response to the policy shock) or about the risk-premium shock.
- The wedge should be applied to every forward-looking variable in the x_exp1 set, not to one equation, or beliefs become inconsistent across equations; IS's discipline comes from one distorted law of motion feeding all forecasts.
- The mean ā is a first-order steady-state shift; with demeaned observables it is not identified, but mean survey biases (Section 3) can pin it down.
- Which bound is the worst case must be checked by monotonicity of the value function in the ambiguous state. That is easy for TFP and less obvious for markup or risk-premium shocks in SW07.
- Asymmetric ambiguity (Baqaee) and endogenous ambiguity from learning (Ilut–Saijo) are not linear wedges; hard for a linear toolbox.

### Gaps
- Posterior estimates and variance shares of the published AER version were not retrieved (the Duke page is behind a bot check). The 2012 draft (27% of GDP at business-cycle frequency) differs from the published abstract; multiplying the published figures (0.70 × 2/3) gives roughly 47% for confidence shocks, which is my arithmetic, not a reported number.
- Ilut–Saijo parameter estimates and the belief specification in the estimated model were not retrieved.
- BIS (2018): sample and estimation method not confirmed from the abstract.
- Masolo–Monti: whether the model is estimated or calibrated was not verified.

## 3. Subjective belief wedges disciplined by survey data

### Takeaway
Bhandari–Borovička–Ho (REStud 2025) is the closest structural template: wedges come from model-consistent pessimism with a time-varying robustness state θ_t, the one-step wedge on any variable is `−θ_t z̄'ψ_wψ_w'v_x'`, and the θ process is calibrated to Michigan wedges (survey forecast minus VAR forecast), which are positive on average, countercyclical and close to one-factor. Bianchi–Ludvigson–Ma (AER 2022) measure distortions as survey minus a real-time machine-learning forecast and find large, time-varying biases but do not embed them in a DSGE; for hetlearn, survey observables plus a factor-structured additive wedge are easy, while BBH's structural loading is hard.

### Cited Findings
- Bhandari, Borovička & Ho (2025), "Survey Data and Subjective Beliefs in Business Cycle Models," *REStud* 92(3): 1375–1437, DOI 10.1093/restud/rdae054 (Richmond Fed WP 19-14, 2019): household beliefs depart from RE as model-consistent pessimism/optimism disciplined by household forecast data; in a model with goods and labour-market frictions, more pessimism raises unemployment and inflation forecasts and lowers activity; linear solution techniques keep time-varying belief distortions — [REStud page](https://www.restud.com/survey-data-and-subjective-beliefs-in-business-cycle-models/); [Richmond Fed WP 19-14](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/RichmondFedOrg/publications/research/working_papers/2019/wp19-14.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fedrwp/19-14.html)
- Evidence (WP 2019): Michigan household forecasts of unemployment and inflation are biased upward on average, both biases rise in recessions, and households forecasting high inflation also forecast high unemployment; corroborated with the NY Fed Survey of Consumer Expectations — [WP 19-14](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/RichmondFedOrg/publications/research/working_papers/2019/wp19-14.pdf)
- Measurement (QuantEcon replication of the published version): `Δ_t(z) = Ẽ_t z_{t+k} − E_t z_{t+k}`; Michigan one-year questions give four-quarter wedges; inflation from the mean Michigan forecast, unemployment from categorical answers via Carlson–Parkin; benchmark E_t from a nine-variable VAR(2) estimated on 1960Q1–2019Q4; SPF as a robustness check; wedge sample 1982Q1–2019Q4; wedge identity `Δ_t(z) = Cov_t(m*_{t+1}, z_{t+1})` — [QuantEcon lecture](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/subjective_beliefs_business_cycles.html)
- Mechanism (WP 2019): pessimism overweights future states with low continuation utility, with dynamically consistent decisions; since continuation utilities depend on actions and prices, beliefs and aggregates are determined jointly, giving overidentifying restrictions; pessimism raises the subjective probability of lower productivity growth, tighter policy and more pessimism; the perturbation scales the belief distortion so it survives the deterministic limit and extends to heterogeneous beliefs (used to separate households from firms) — [WP 19-14](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/RichmondFedOrg/publications/research/working_papers/2019/wp19-14.pdf)
- Calibration, not likelihood estimation (WP 2019): most parameters calibrated; TFP process estimated from Fernald data; policy and wage parameters set to match VAR impulse responses; the θ process is calibrated to the wedges: the first principal component explains 76% of wedge variation, ρ_θ = 0.714 (autocorrelation of that component), θ̄ = 5.64 and σ_θ = 4.3 to fit wedge means and volatilities — [WP 19-14](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/RichmondFedOrg/publications/research/working_papers/2019/wp19-14.pdf)
- Fit (WP 2019, data 1982Q1–2015Q4, annualised %): mean inflation wedge 1.25 (data) vs 0.90 (model); mean unemployment wedge 0.54 vs 0.55; inflation-wedge volatility 1.03 vs 0.73; unemployment-wedge volatility 0.45 vs 0.45; unemployment volatility 1.65 (data), 1.39 (benchmark), 0.55 without θ_t shocks, i.e. the RE version reproduces the unemployment volatility puzzle; without TFP shocks the inflation wedge has a negative mean (−0.32) and correlates negatively with the unemployment wedge, which is counterfactual; with rational firms the inflation bias is much smaller — [WP 19-14](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/RichmondFedOrg/publications/research/working_papers/2019/wp19-14.pdf)
- The QuantEcon lecture reports the published paper's first-component share as 78.6% (about 79–81% across normalisations) and empirical mean wedges of 0.52 pp (unemployment) and 1.22 pp (inflation); its five-state emulator (belief shock = 84.3% of unemployment variance) is calibrated to selected moments, not solved from the structural model — [QuantEcon lecture](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/subjective_beliefs_business_cycles.html)
- Bianchi, Ludvigson & Ma (2022), "Belief Distortions and Macroeconomic Fluctuations," *AER* 112(7): 2269–2315: a belief distortion is a systematic ex ante error from misweighting available information; the benchmark is a real-time machine forecast from a regression of the outcome on the respondent's own forecast plus hundreds of real-time predictors (diffusion-index factors), estimated by elastic net; "no distortion" means `β_F = 1, B = 0, α = 0` — [AER PDF](https://www.sydneyludvigson.com/s/biases_aer.pdf); [NBER w27406](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27406)
- BLM method details: elastic net performed best, then lasso, while random forest and empirical-Bayes regression performed poorly; surveys are SPF, Michigan SOC and Blue Chip; evaluation samples SPF/SOC 1995:I–2018:II, Blue Chip 1997:III–2018:II (inflation) and 1996:I–2018:II (GDP) — [AER PDF](https://www.sydneyludvigson.com/s/biases_aer.pdf)
- BLM findings: all respondent types overweight the judgemental component; median inflation forecasts in all surveys are biased upward ("pessimistic"); growth forecasts of professionals and executives are optimistic, households slightly pessimistic; in 2013:II–2018:II the median SPF growth forecast was biased up by 38% of actual GDP growth (18% less accurate than the machine) and SPF inflation forecasts were 37% less accurate; little out-of-sample evidence that lagged forecast revisions predict average forecast errors; benchmarks built with ex post data overstate distortions — [AER PDF](https://www.sydneyludvigson.com/s/biases_aer.pdf)
- BLM dynamics: after the cyclical shocks of Angeletos–Collard–Dellas used by Angeletos–Huo–Sastry, SPF forecasts first underreact (more so to the output shock) and later overreact (especially to the inflation shock) relative to the machine; comparing with realised outcomes overstates over- and underreaction — [AER PDF](https://www.sydneyludvigson.com/s/biases_aer.pdf)
- Forecast data as DSGE observables for news shocks: Miyamoto & Nguyen (2020), *JME* 116: 184–200, US 1955Q1–2006Q4: expectations data make news shocks much more precisely estimated, and long-horizon news moves output only modestly before realisation — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/eee/moneco/v116y2020icp184-200.html). Hirose & Kurozumi (*Macroeconomic Dynamics*, online 2019, 2021 issue): with forecast data in an NK DSGE, technology news shocks account for a large share of US output-growth fluctuations and estimates are more precise — [Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/macroeconomic-dynamics/article/identifying-news-shocks-with-forecast-data/E50C8B80B0A4E7EB2421FBC73C0AA9E2); [Dallas Fed WP 366](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/international/wpapers/2019/0366)
- Subjective price beliefs: Adam, Marcet & Beutel (2017, *AER*) find survey capital-gains expectations optimistic at peaks and pessimistic at troughs, which RE cannot match — [UCL copy](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10069199/1/Stock%20Price%20Booms_Adam.pdf); Adam & Merkel (2019, ECB WP 2316 / CEPR DP 13866) add survey-based extrapolative stock-price beliefs to a business-cycle model and obtain boom–bust cycles and a financial accelerator without financial frictions — [ECB WP 2316](https://ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecb.wp2316~4effe6153e.es.pdf); [CEPR DP 13866](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP13866.pdf)

### Inferences
- Survey data enter in two ways, both compatible with hetlearn: (i) as observables in the measurement equation (Milani; the IJCB paper), which identify the type mix and any free wedge; (ii) as moments that calibrate a structural wedge process (BBH) or a dispersion proxy for ambiguity (IS). Option (i) with an AR(1)/VAR(1) wedge is easy and is the estimation counterpart of BBH's reduced-form emulator.
- A "reduced-form BBH" module: a one-factor wedge `w_t = μ + λf_t` across the expectation variables (π, u or y), f_t AR(1). λ free and estimated from survey observables is easy. λ restricted to the structural direction `−z̄'ψ_wψ_w'v_x'` is hard: v_x needs the first-order approximation of lifetime utility in SW07 variables (habit-adjusted consumption, hours) plus BBH's modified Riccati and Blanchard–Kahn fixed point, repeated each quarter.
- BBH's result that the sign of the inflation wedge requires supply shocks means a structural module in SW07 would need TFP or markup shocks that load on the worst case; the wedge direction becomes a testable output, not a free parameter.
- BLM's survey-minus-machine series could serve as a direct observable for the wedge, which removes the model-implied RE forecast from that measurement equation; the cost is data construction (medium).
- Mean wedges are large relative to their volatility (inflation about 1.2 pp mean against about 1 pp volatility in BBH); a nonzero wedge mean is needed whenever survey levels, not demeaned series, enter the likelihood.

## 4. Expectation, sentiment and confidence shocks: rules or shocks, and nesting in x_exp1

### Takeaway
Milani's expectation shocks (2011) and sentiment shocks (2017) are literally an additive wedge on a constant-gain learning forecast, identified by SPF observables; they account for about half of US output fluctuations (2011) and for over 40% of output and consumption and about 60% of investment and inflation variability (2017, WP version). Angeletos–Collard–Dellas confidence is a higher-order-belief bias that enters linear models as an exogenous AR(1) state with model-determined loadings (51–55% of business-cycle output variance), whereas Lorenzoni/BLL noise, Barsky–Sims, Beaudry–Portier news and the Angeletos–La'O and Benhabib–Wang–Wen sentiments are information or equilibrium structures under RE, not expectation-formation rules, and can only be mimicked by wedges.

### Cited Findings
- Milani (2011), "Expectation Shocks and Learning as Drivers of the Business Cycle," *Economic Journal* 121(552): 379–401, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2011.02422.x (CEPR DP 7743): observed expectations replace RE in an estimated NK model with constant-gain learning; expectation shocks are deviations of observed expectations from the learning forecast and capture optimism/pessimism; shocks to expected real activity account for roughly half of US business-cycle fluctuations — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v121y2011i552p379-401.html); [author PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~fmilani/ESL.pdf)
- Milani (2011) expectation equation: `Ê_{t−1}[y_{t+1}, π_{t+1}, i_{t+1}]' = a_{t−1} + b_{t−1}Ê_{t−1}[y_t, π_t, i_t]' + [e^{y1}_{t−1}, e^π_{t−1}, 0]'`; observables `Ê_{t−1}y_t, Ê_{t−1}y_{t+1}, Ê_{t−1}π_{t+1}, Ê_{t−1}i_t` (SPF where available); e^π and e^i are AR(1) with nonzero means, the two output-expectation shocks a VAR(1); Bayesian estimation on 1968:IV–2009:I with pre-sample 1947:I–1968:IV; constant-gain posterior 0.0196 — [author PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~fmilani/ESL.pdf)

- Milani (2017), "Sentiment and the U.S. business cycle," is in *JEDC* 82 (2017), starting p. 289, not the JME — [Crossref citation list via Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/1FFCA6077C5FE17EFC151947B6BBB292); WP versions [UCI 14-15-04](https://www.economics.uci.edu/files/docs/workingpapers/2014-15/14-15-04.pdf), [revised PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~fmilani/ASBCr.pdf)

- Angeletos, Collard & Dellas (2018), "Quantifying Confidence," *Econometrica* 86(5): 1689–1726, DOI 10.3982/ECTA13079: a tractable method for autonomous variation in higher-order beliefs; waves of optimism and pessimism about the short-term outlook, unlike news about medium- to long-run prospects — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/doi/10.3982/ECTA13079)
- ACD mechanism (NBER WP 20807, revised version that thanks the Econometrica editor): each island sees `z_it = log A_t + ε_it`, believes its own error is N(0, σ²) but others' errors are N(ξ_t, σ²) ("agree to disagree"), with σ → 0; `ξ_t = ρξ_{t−1} + ζ_t`; policy rule `X_t = G(K_t, A_t, ξ_t)`; cross-equation restrictions tie beliefs about different endogenous objects together, unlike "completely" irrational expectations — [NBER WP 20807](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20807.pdf)
- ACD for a generic linear DSGE: stage-1 beliefs `E_it[s_t] = z_it` and `E_it[E_jt s_t] = E_it[z_jt] = z_it + Δξ_t`, with the truth revealed in stage 2; solution `X_t = Θ_X X^b_t + Θ_s s_t + Θ_ξ ξ_t` obtained as a transformation of the RE solution, so the augmented model "can be simulated, calibrated, and estimated with essentially the same facility as the original one" — [NBER WP 20807](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20807.pdf)

- ACD variance shares at 6–32 quarters (Table 2, %): flexible prices Y 54.72, C 70.21, I 41.60, N 68.32; sticky prices Y 51.28, C 61.95, I 38.50, N 64.15, π 11.64, R 40.84; the shock accounts for more than 100% of the hours–consumption covariance — [NBER WP 20807](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20807.pdf)
- Lorenzoni (2009), "A Theory of Demand Shocks," *AER* 99(5): 2050–2084: agents see their own productivity and a noisy public signal of aggregate productivity; noise shocks behave like demand shocks (raise output, employment and inflation in the short run, no long-run effect); a calibrated version gives realistic short-run volatility — [NBER w12477](https://nber.org/papers/w12477); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/51815)

- Barsky & Sims (2012), "Information, Animal Spirits, and the Meaning of Innovations in Consumer Confidence," *AER* 102(4): 1343–1377: indirect inference with an NK model; confidence innovations mainly carry news about future productivity growth (the information view), not animal spirits — [NBER w15049](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15049.pdf); [openICPSR](https://www.openicpsr.org/openicpsr/project/112525)

- An unrestricted Milani wedge absorbs a lot (40–54% of output variance). With hetlearn's endogenous shares, some of that may be explained by switching between RE and BR forecasts; a joint estimation with both is the natural test (an expectation, not a result).
- Identification in hetlearn: the free wedge competes with share movements and survey measurement error. Persistence (AR wedge vs iid error), a common factor across variables (BBH), and cross-equation restrictions (IS, ACD) all help; ACD's point that disciplined belief shocks tie expectations of different endogenous variables together argues against fully independent wedges per variable.
- With anchored RE agents the wedge in the BR forecast does not feed into RE forecasts; with model-consistent RE agents, RE agents anticipate others' biased expectations, which is a higher-order-belief channel close to ACD. This choice changes the propagation of sentiment shocks and should be an explicit option.

## 5. Optimism/pessimism and overconfidence as systematic biases

### Takeaway
De Grauwe's model is a pair of biased heuristics (`Ẽ^opt_t y_{t+1} = g_t`, `Ẽ^pes_t y_{t+1} = −g_t`, `g_t = (β + δ·var(y_t))/2`) with Brock–Hommes switching on discounted squared errors, and for inflation a target-anchored rule against an extrapolative one; this maps directly onto hetlearn's BR types and discrete choice (easy). Systematic bias also shows up in ambiguity (worst-case TFP 0.435% lower in the IS draft), in BBH and BLM (upward-biased inflation forecasts), and in ACD (agents overestimate confidence effects), while overconfidence in the Broer–Kohlhas sense concerns the precision of private information and needs a dispersed-information structure.

### Cited Findings
- De Grauwe (2011), "Animal spirits and monetary policy," *Economic Theory* 47(2–3): 423–457, DOI 10.1007/s00199-010-0543-0: agents use simple biased heuristics and adapt them by learning from errors; correlation of biased beliefs produces waves of optimism and pessimism; strict inflation targeting is suboptimal because it leaves more room for these waves — [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/45124/); [CESifo WP 2418 (2008)](https://www.cesifo.org/en/publications/2008/working-paper/animal-spirits-and-monetary-policy)
- Rules (CESifo WP 2418): optimists `Ẽ^opt_t y_{t+1} = g_t`, pessimists `Ẽ^pes_t y_{t+1} = −g_t`, `g_t = d_t/2`, divergence `d_t = β + δ var(y_t)` with β, δ ≥ 0 (δ = 0 gives a constant bias β/2); inflation "targeters" `Ẽ^tar_t π_{t+1} = π*` and "extrapolators" `Ẽ^ext_t π_{t+1} = π_{t−1}`; fitness `U_{opt,t} = −Σ_k ω_k[y_{t−k−1} − Ẽ^opt_{t−k−2}y_{t−k−1}]²` with geometrically declining ω_k; shares `α_{opt,t} = exp(γU_{opt,t})/[exp(γU_{opt,t}) + exp(γU_{pes,t})]` following Brock–Hommes, with γ = ∞ selecting only the best rule; the heuristics are imposed after deriving the demand and supply equations, as in the learning literature — [CESifo WP 2418 PDF](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp2418.pdf)
- In the WP simulations the extrapolator share fluctuates around 50%, with episodes in which extrapolators dominate, read as a loss of confidence in the inflation target — [CESifo WP 2418 PDF](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp2418.pdf)
- De Grauwe & Ji (2020), "Structural reforms, animal spirits, and monetary policies," *European Economic Review* 124: 103395 — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eecrev/v124y2020ics0014292120300271.html)
- Broer & Kohlhas (2024), "Forecaster (Mis-)Behavior," *REStat* 106(5): 1334–1351, DOI 10.1162/rest_a_01210: SPF forecasters revise too much; excess revisions mask both over- and underreaction to public news; overconfidence in private information reproduces both, which noisy rational expectations and strategic diversification do not — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v106y2024i5p1334-1351.html); [ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:60220496-b173-41b0-9724-1222cafe1cbf)
- Average biases in data: median inflation forecasts biased upward in SPF, SOC and Blue Chip, professional growth forecasts optimistic — [BLM AER PDF](https://www.sydneyludvigson.com/s/biases_aer.pdf); Michigan unemployment and inflation forecasts biased upward and countercyclical — [BBH WP 19-14](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/RichmondFedOrg/publications/research/working_papers/2019/wp19-14.pdf)
- Average biases in models: IS worst-case one-step TFP 0.435% below truth (2012 draft) — [NBER conference draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf); ACD agents overestimate the size of ξ's effects and do not learn — [NBER WP 20807](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20807.pdf)

### Inferences
- De Grauwe's output rules are constants (or volatility-scaled constants) around the steady state, not deviations from a model forecast. In hetlearn: `E^opt_t x_{t+1} = E^base_t x_{t+1} + g_t`, `E^pes_t x_{t+1} = E^base_t x_{t+1} − g_t`, with E^base the steady state (De Grauwe) or the learner's forecast (a "biased learner"). Fitness and logit already exist in hetlearn; the additions are the bias rule and, if δ > 0, a rolling variance of the output gap. Easy.
- Targeters vs extrapolators is a credibility block, the same structure as Hommes–Lustenhouwer (Section 7). Easy.
- A symmetric optimist/pessimist pair gives zero average bias when shares balance, so matching the positive mean survey biases (BLM, BBH) needs asymmetric biases or a separate constant wedge.
- Overconfidence requires private signals and their perceived precision (dispersed information), so it is not a representative-mix wedge; medium to hard, and it overlaps with the noisy-information module covered by another researcher.

## 6. Anticipated utility, per-period re-solving and Bayesian model averaging

### Takeaway
Kreps's anticipated utility (update beliefs by Bayes or least squares, then act as if current beliefs are permanent) is the standard justification for re-solving the model every period; Cogley–Sargent (IER 2008) benchmark it against fully Bayesian decisions, and estimated learning DSGEs such as Milani (2017) invoke it explicitly. Bayesian model averaging appears on the policy side (Cogley–Sargent 2005), in private-agent learning (Evans–Honkapohja–Sargent–Williams; Cho–Kasa) and in robust form (Hansen–Sargent 2010); fully Bayesian learning about regimes with forward-looking agents is tractable in Markov-switching DSGE (Bianchi–Melosi 2016), while a BMA type is a special case of hetlearn's discrete choice.

### Cited Findings
- Kreps (1998), "Anticipated utility and dynamic choice," in Jacobs, Kalai & Kamien (eds.), *Frontiers of Research in Economic Theory: The Nancy L. Schwartz Memorial Lectures, 1983–1997*, Cambridge University Press, 1998 (Econometric Society Monographs 29); volume confirmed, chapter pages not — [TUM catalogue](https://search.ub.tum.de/vufind/Record/DE-604.BV013359399)
- Cogley & Sargent (2008), "Anticipated utility and rational expectations as approximations of Bayesian decision making," *IER* 49(1): 185–221: a Markov decision problem with unknown transition probabilities as hidden states; compares the RE approximation (agent knows the probabilities) and Kreps's anticipated utility (Bayesian updating, decisions myopic with respect to future belief revisions) with the Bayesian rule — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ier/iecrev/v49y2008i1p185-221.html)
- Use in an estimated DSGE: in Milani (2017) agents maximise expected lifetime utility treating current beliefs and the PLM as constant, which rules out active experimentation and is "standard in the adaptive learning literature" — [Milani revised WP](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~fmilani/ASBCr.pdf)
- Cogley & Sargent (2005), "The conquest of US inflation: Learning and robustness to model uncertainty," *RED* 8(2): 528–563: each period the central bank sets the systematic part of inflation using updated probabilities over three competing Phillips-curve models; caution induced by model uncertainty explains why it presided over 1970s inflation even after the data favoured the natural-rate model — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/red/issued/v8y2005i2p528-563.html); [ECB WP 478](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecb:ecbwps:20050478)
- Bianchi & Melosi (2016), "Modeling the evolution of expectations and uncertainty in general equilibrium," *IER* 57(2): 717–756, DOI 10.1111/iere.12174: solution methods for GE models with forward-looking agents whose beliefs move in waves of optimism, pessimism and uncertainty; agents are fully rational Bayesian learners who take into account how their beliefs will change; covers gradual and abrupt shifts; RBC illustration — [Duke](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/3401c057-2c00-441e-b56e-852a32e8d4a4); [Chicago Fed WP 2013-12](https://www.Chicagofed.Org/digital_assets/publications/working_papers/2013/wp2013_12.pdf)
- Evans, Honkapohja, Sargent & Williams (2013), "Bayesian Model Averaging, Learning, and Model Selection," in Sargent & Vilmunen (eds.), *Macroeconomics at the Service of Public Policy*, OUP, pp. 99–119: agents average an RE forecast and a time-varying-parameter model by Bayes; learning eventually selects one, and for some parameters the non-RE model wins, depending on the strength of expectational feedback in a Muth model — [St Andrews portal](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/bayesian-model-averaging-learning-and-model-selection/); [WP PDF](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~nwilliam/learnbayes.pdf)
- Cho & Kasa (2017), "Gresham's Law of Model Averaging," *AER* 107(11): 3589–3616, DOI 10.1257/aer.20160665: a decision maker averages a TVP model and a constant-parameter model; the true parameters are constant but expectational feedback lets fears of instability confirm themselves — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v107y2017i11p3589-3616.html)
- Adam & Marcet (2011), "Internal rationality, imperfect market knowledge and asset prices," *JET* 146(3), pp. 1224–1252 (Mannheim lists 1244–1252), DOI 10.1016/j.jet.2010.11.003: agents maximise under dynamically consistent subjective beliefs about variables beyond their control (internally rational) without knowing the true process (not externally rational); in asset pricing, learning about prices matters for outcomes — [LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/36856); [Mannheim](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/31901/)
- Robust Bayesian averaging: Hansen & Sargent (2010) (Section 1) — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/quante/v1y2010i1p129-162.html)

### Inferences
- hetlearn's quarter-by-quarter re-solving under current beliefs is Kreps-style anticipated utility; Cogley–Sargent (2008) is the citation for when it approximates the Bayesian rule, and Milani (2017) is a precedent in an estimated SW-type model. Internal rationality (Adam–Marcet) is the alternative foundation in which agents' optimisation itself uses subjective price beliefs (closer to infinite-horizon learning, covered elsewhere).
- With Gaussian forecast errors and known variances, Bayesian posterior weights are `ω_{i,t} ∝ ω_{i,0}·exp(−Σ_s e²_{i,s}/(2σ_i²))`, a logit in cumulative squared errors with intensity 1/(2σ²) and no forgetting (my derivation). hetlearn's discrete choice on discounted squared errors therefore nests a "BMA type" as the case memory → 1 with γ tied to the error variance. Easy.
- Fully Bayesian regime learning with agents anticipating their own belief revisions (Bianchi–Melosi) needs a Markov-switching solution; outside the current architecture (hard). Policymaker BMA (Cogley–Sargent 2005) is a policy-side module, not private expectations.
- Cho–Kasa and EHSW show that averaging over misspecified models can select a non-RE model in self-referential economies, i.e. the selection channel resembles heuristic switching (covered elsewhere).

### Gaps
- Cogley–Sargent (2008) quantitative conclusions on the accuracy of anticipated utility were not read.
- Kreps chapter pages not verified.
- No likelihood-estimated DSGE with private-agent BMA on US data was found in this pass.

## 7. Sweep: remaining bounded-rationality families

### Takeaway
Beyond the families covered by other researchers, the sweep found internal rationality, belief functions in indeterminate models, narratives measured from text, news selection, central-bank communication under learning, endogenous credibility, imprecise memory, as-if discounting from noisy simulation, restricted-complexity ("simple") models, imperfect problem solving, tail-risk learning, machine-learning and LLM belief benchmarks, and the temporary-equilibrium framing. The near-term toolbox candidates are a target-anchored credibility type, a communicated or dimension-restricted PLM for the learner, and text- or ML-based indices used as observables for a wedge; the rest are nonlinear or heterogeneous-agent mechanisms.

### Cited Findings
- Internal rationality (Adam & Marcet 2011): agents optimise given dynamically consistent subjective beliefs, without knowing the true price process — [LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/36856)
- Belief function (Farmer & Nicolò 2018): an equation for expected nominal GDP growth replaces the missing equilibrium condition of an indeterminate model — [VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/blogs-and-reviews/keynesian-economics-without-phillips-curve)
- Narratives as contagious beliefs: Flynn & Sastry (2024), "The Macroeconomics of Narratives," NBER WP 32602: textual proxies for narratives from 10-K filings via NLP; narratives explain 19% of output variance, 32% of the early-2000s output decline and 18% of the Great Recession decline (NBER version; figures may differ across drafts); overall optimism is not contagious enough to generate hysteresis, some granular narratives are (thesis version) — [NBER w32602](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32602); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/151338?show=full); [VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/macroeconomics-narratives)
- Narratives as directed acyclic graphs: Macaulay & Song (Oxford WP 973, 2022; Bank of Canada SWP 2023-23): competing narratives in coverage of the 2019 US yield-curve inversion; the recessionary narrative goes with more pessimistic Twitter sentiment; for 2021, a viral narrative of inflation harming the real economy accounts for 42% of the fall in consumer sentiment in the second half of the year; the BoC version adds a financial-frictions model — [BoC SWP 2023-23](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2023/04/staff-working-paper-2023-23/); [MPRA](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/113620); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oxf:wpaper:973)
- Subjective models of the economy: Andre, Pizzinelli, Roth & Wohlfart (2022), *REStud* 89: 2958–2991, DOI 10.1093/restud/rdac008: 6,500 US households and 1,500 experts given the same shock information disagree widely, even on the sign of effects; households and experts differ on average; differences link to which propagation channels come to mind (associative memory) — [FLEX record](https://flex.uni-frankfurt.de/index.php/publications/subjective-models-of-the-macroeconomy-evidence-from-experts-and-representative-samples/); [CESifo WP 7850](https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/cesifo1_wp7850.pdf). Earlier drafts: households agree in direction with experts for oil-supply and government-spending shocks but not for monetary-policy and income-tax shocks — [Warwick TWERP 1342](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/research/workingpapers/2021/twerp_1342_-_roth.pdf)
- News selection: Nimark (2014), "Man-Bites-Dog Business Cycles," *AER* 104(8): 2320–2367: newsworthiness depends on how unusual an event is; signals that arrive after rare events raise uncertainty and disagreement; the model is estimated with likelihood methods — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v104y2014i8p2320-67.html)
- Communication under learning: Eusepi & Preston (2010), "Central Bank Communication and Expectations Stabilization," *AEJ: Macroeconomics* 2(3): 235–271: the Taylor principle alone does not ensure stability under learning; communicating the policy rule, or the variables it responds to, restores it; announcing only the inflation target does not protect against expectations-driven fluctuations when shocks are persistent — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/aea/aejmac/v2y2010i3p235-71.html); [NBER w13259](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13259)
- Endogenous credibility: Hommes & Lustenhouwer (2019), "Inflation targeting and liquidity traps under endogenous credibility," *JME* 107: 48–62, DOI 10.1016/j.jmoneco.2019.01.027: credibility evolves with the central bank's track record; with heterogeneous boundedly rational expectations the stabilising policy set is wider than under RE, but at the ZLB self-fulfilling deflationary spirals can arise depending on credibility — [UvA PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/44671470/1_s2.0_S0304393219300170_main.pdf); [BoC SWP 2019-9](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/swp2019-9.pdf)
- Imprecise memory: da Silveira, Sung & Woodford, "Optimally Imprecise Memory and Biased Forecasts," NBER WP 28075 (Nov 2020, rev. Dec 2023; CEPR DP 15459): memory complexity bounded by mutual information; forecasts vary randomly, average beliefs stay biased in the long run, recent news is overweighted; one listing says AER 2024, unconfirmed — [NBER w28075](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28075/w28075.pdf); [CEPR DP 15459](https://cepr.org/publications/DP15459)
- As-if discounting: Gabaix & Laibson, "Myopia and Discounting," NBER WP 23254 (2017, rev. 2022): patient agents who form expectations from noisy unbiased simulations combined with priors behave as if discounting; the as-if discount function is hyperbolic, `D(t) = 1/(1 + αt)`, when simulation noise variance grows linearly with horizon — [NBER w23254](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23254.pdf)
- Restricted model complexity: Molavi, "Simple Models and Biased Forecasts" (working paper; arXiv 2202.06921; 2023 and 2025 versions): agents restricted to state-space models with at most d states pick the best such model; this adds persistence and comovement, weakens forward guidance in the NK model and makes RBC consumption more sluggish — [arXiv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2202.06921); [Kellogg page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2025/simple-models-and-biased-forecasts). - Imperfect problem solving: Ilut & Valchev (2023), "Economic Agents as Imperfect Problem Solvers," *QJE* 138(1): 313–362, DOI 10.1093/qje/qjac027: costly reasoning to learn the optimal policy function; agents reason more in unusual states, giving state- and history-dependent behaviour, high MPCs and more persistent hand-to-mouth status — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1582279); [NBER w27820](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27820)
- Tail-risk learning ("scarring"): Kozlowski, Veldkamp & Venkateswaran, "The Tail That Wags the Economy: Beliefs and Persistent Stagnation" (JPE 2020 per EconPapers listing; NBER WP 21719): agents estimate the shock distribution from data, so a rare event persistently raises perceived tail risk and depresses investment and output — [NBER w21719](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21719/w21719.pdf); [NBER Reporter 2019](https://www.nber.org/reporter/2019number3/beliefs-tail-risk-and-secular-stagnation)
- Machine-learning benchmark: Bianchi, Ludvigson & Ma (2022) (Section 3) — [AER PDF](https://www.sydneyludvigson.com/s/biases_aer.pdf)
- LLM-generated expectations: Bybee (working paper, 2023): GPT-3.5 prompted with historical Wall Street Journal articles produces expectations that track the SPF and other surveys and share their deviations from full-information RE — [NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f194779/f194779.pdf); [arXiv 2305.02823 record](https://isidore.science/document/2305.02823)
- Temporary equilibrium: Woodford (2013), "Macroeconomic Analysis without the Rational Expectations Hypothesis," *Annual Review of Economics* 5: 303–346: a temporary-equilibrium framework that admits many expectation specifications and reduces to the NK model under RE; compares rationalisable equilibrium, restricted perceptions, least-squares learning, rational beliefs and near-rational expectations — [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/abs/10.1146/annurev-economics-080511-110857); [NBER w19368](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19368)

### Inferences
- Plausibility as hetlearn modules (my assessment):
  - Target-anchored / credibility type (De Grauwe targeters; Hommes–Lustenhouwer): `E^CB_t π_{t+1} = π*`, with credibility = its share under the existing switching. Easy.
  - Communication (Eusepi–Preston): an option for the RLS learner's PLM to include the announced rule's variables. Medium.
  - Restricted-complexity PLM (Molavi): requires a latent-state learner (Kalman-based) rather than RLS on observables. Medium.
  - Narratives: medium as an observable for a wedge (a text-based index loading on w_t); hard as a structural contagion model.
  - Machine-learning distortions (BLM): medium, as a wedge observable.
  - LLM expectations: research stage; no DSGE implementation found.
  - Imprecise memory, imperfect problem solving, tail-risk learning: nonlinear or heterogeneous-agent mechanisms; not near-term for a linear representative-mix toolbox.
  - As-if discounting (Gabaix–Laibson): formally a horizon-dependent shrinkage of expected deviations; reuse the cognitive-discounting module (covered elsewhere).
  - Temporary equilibrium: hetlearn already solves a temporary equilibrium each quarter; Woodford (2013) is the citation for that framing.
- Covered by other researchers and not repeated here: Gabaix behavioural inattention, k-step lookahead (finite planning horizons), endogenous-gain anchoring, experience-based learning.
- Present bias (quasi-hyperbolic discounting) is a preference, not an expectation rule; only the Gabaix–Laibson channel gives an expectation-based route to the same behaviour.

## 8. Mapping to the hetlearn toolbox

### Takeaway
Almost every family reduces to one of four components: (1) an additive wedge in x_exp1 with its own AR/VAR process, identified by survey observables (Milani; free-loading BBH; text or ML indices), which is easy; (2) a wedge whose loading comes from the current solution (Ilut–Schneider ambiguity about TFP or policy; an ACD approximation), which is medium; (3) an extra BR type in the discrete-choice pool (De Grauwe optimists/pessimists, a central-bank-target type, a naive belief function, BMA weights, restricted-dimension PLMs), easy to medium; (4) a value-function-based worst case (Hansen–Sargent LQ, structural BBH), which is hard. All of (1)–(3) keep a linear Gaussian state space, so the time-varying Kalman filter likelihood and Bayesian estimation carry over.

### Cited Findings
- Ambiguity about conditional means "can still be solved and estimated using first-order approximations"; IS estimate with standard Bayesian methods on the linear state space — [IS 2012 draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf)
- The ACD belief augmentation adds only ξ_t to the state and delivers the solution as a transformation of the original solution — [NBER WP 20807](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20807.pdf)
- BBH's perturbation yields a linear solution with a modified Blanchard–Kahn system and extends to heterogeneous subjective beliefs — [QuantEcon lecture](https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/subjective_beliefs_business_cycles.html); [WP 19-14](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/RichmondFedOrg/publications/research/working_papers/2019/wp19-14.pdf)
- Robust-control solution code (Matlab/Octave/Gauss) for forward-looking models exists — [Giordani & Söderlind abstract](https://swopec.hhs.se/hastef/abs/hastef0499.htm)
- Survey observable timing used in an estimated SW-type learning model: t−1 information, SPF one-quarter-ahead columns, real-time data, anticipated utility — [Milani 2017 revised WP](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~fmilani/ASBCr.pdf)
- Survey dispersion (SPF interquartile range of real GDP growth and inflation) has been used as the data counterpart of ambiguity — [IS 2012 draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f59669/f59669.pdf); Blue Chip-based ambiguity for monetary policy — [Masolo & Monti](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/handle/10807/227728)

### Inferences
General form (my proposal): `x_exp1_t = Σ_i n_{i,t}(E^i_t x_{t+1} + w^i_t)`, `w^i_t = μ_i + Λ_{i,t} f_t`, `f_t = R f_{t−1} + η_t`. Special cases: Milani (Λ = selection matrix d, f = α_t); reduced-form BBH (one factor, Λ free); Ilut–Schneider (Λ_t = −B_Z(t), the TFP impact column of the current solution; f = a_t; μ from ā); De Grauwe (no f, μ = ±g_t inside two BR types).

Implementation notes (my reading):
- Kalman filter: wedge factors are extra exogenous states; when loadings depend on the re-solved model (IS), the transition matrix becomes time varying, as it already is under BR beliefs, so the time-varying filter applies unchanged.
- Anchored vs model-consistent RE agents: if RE agents forecast with an RE solution that includes the wedge process, they anticipate others' biased expectations (an ACD-like higher-order channel); if they are anchored to a solution without wedges, they ignore it. Make this a switch; it changes how sentiment shocks propagate.
- Survey horizons: SPF one-quarter-ahead series match `x_exp1` directly with t−1 timing (Milani); Michigan one-year questions (BBH) need four-quarter forecasts, i.e. iterating each type's PLM and the wedge factor (`Σ_h` loadings × `E_t f_{t+h}`).
- Means: wedge means map to average survey biases (inflation about 1.2 pp in BBH; upward bias in BLM); if observables are demeaned, wedge means drop out; if survey levels are used, estimate μ.
- Steady state: a constant wedge (IS ā, BBH θ̄) shifts the steady state at first order; with a model linearised around the RE steady state this enters as a constant term in the expectation equations.
- Consistency: apply belief-distortion wedges (IS, BBH) to all forward-looking variables in the x_exp1 set from one distorted law of motion, rather than as independent shocks per equation; free per-variable wedges (Milani) are the reduced-form alternative and lose those cross-equation restrictions.
- Suggested order of work: (1) Milani-type wedge with survey observables; (2) De Grauwe and central-bank-target types; (3) BMA weights as a switching variant; (4) Ilut–Schneider wedge with model-implied loading and dispersion observable; (5) structural BBH; (6) Hansen–Sargent LQ robustness.

- Whether the IS wedge equivalence (`−ν_Z a_t`) survives when only a share of agents is ambiguity averse and RE agents are model-consistent was not checked; Ilut–Krivenko–Schneider's heterogeneous-belief perturbation is the reference to consult.
- The mapping of ACD's two-stage timing into SW07's single-stage equations was not worked out.
